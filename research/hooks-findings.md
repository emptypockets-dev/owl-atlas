# Shareable-fact research: ranked hooks

Research and drafting only. No site source file was touched. Machine-readable
companion: `research/hooks-findings.json` (hook records plus 13 new source
records in the exact `src/content.json` `sources` shape, `accessed` 2026-09-21).

**House rules applied to every line below.** No claim is phrased more strongly
than the record that was actually opened. Debated or approximate chronology
stays approximate. Citing a museum implies no endorsement. Nothing here is
authentication advice. And "Mint State after 2,400 years" stays attached to the
one coin in `src/one-owl.json` (NGC 2086328-049) — it is never a fact about owls
as a class.

Every source below was downloaded and read during this research. Where something
could only be found in a search-result summary, it is in the "Could not verify"
section, not here. One widely-quoted sentence had to be thrown out for exactly
that reason — see item 5 of that section.

## The ten best hooks

### 1. One owl was four days' pay on the Acropolis

> **Hook.** On the Acropolis in 408/7 BC, one owl was about four days' pay.
>
> **Expansion.** The building accounts for the Erechtheion record the wages of
> the people finishing the temple, and Attic Inscriptions Online summarises the
> rate they worked to: a day-wage of one drachma, or piece-rates worked out from
> it. A tetradrachm was four drachmas, so a single owl stood roughly for a
> four-day week of that work.

**Source.** `hook-erechtheion` — *IG* I³ 476, Erechtheion building accounts,
408/7 BC, AIO, translation Stephen Lambert & Robin Osborne.
<https://www.atticinscriptions.com/inscription/IGI3/476>. AIO's note 6 states
that the architect and the labourers, skilled and unskilled, were employed
either on a day-wage of 1 dr. or on piece-rates computed on the basis of that
wage. Denomination from the existing `met` record.

**Caveats.** Most surviving entries are piece-rates for named tasks, not counted
days — the 1 dr. day-wage is AIO's summary of how the rates were computed, not a
quoted line of the stone. One year, one building, in the last years of the
Peloponnesian War. And nothing says anyone was handed a tetradrachm: the
equivalence is arithmetic on the denomination.

**Why it leads.** Primary document, on a site the atlas already cites, arriving
at a number a stranger can hold in one hand. No conversion chain.

### 2. A cut-open fake became the property of a goddess

> **Hook.** An Athenian law of 375/4 BC ordered that a fake owl be cut open on
> the spot and handed over to a goddess.
>
> **Expansion.** Nikophon's law put a public approver among the bankers' tables
> in the Agora and by the stele of Poseidon in Piraeus, and told him what to do
> with a coin that was not what it claimed. A piece with a bronze or lead core
> was to be cut through at once, and then became sacred property of the Mother
> of the Gods, deposited with the Council.

**Source.** Existing site source `law` — Law on approvers (dokimastai) of silver
coinage, 375/4 BC (AIO 819 / RO 25), translation Stephen Lambert & P. J. Rhodes.
<https://atticinscriptions.com/inscription/RO/25>. Verified this session: the
approver sits among the tables (lines 4–5) and by the stele of Poseidon in
Piraeus (lines 36–41); a bronze- or lead-cored coin is cut through immediately
and becomes sacred property of the Mother of the Gods, deposited with the
Council (lines 10–13).

**Caveats.** A document of 375/4 BC, not a description of every year. The clause
about foreign silver struck with an Athenian type is partly restored, and AIO
flags the restoration as plausible but without parallels — build nothing on it.
This is a rule about what officials did, not a method a reader can apply.

**Note.** The site already carries this source for the glossary and the eastern-
imitation discussion. The confiscation-to-the-goddess clause is not yet used
anywhere, and it is the best untapped line in the existing bibliography.

### 3. The owl in your pocket

> **Hook.** The owl on the Greek one-euro coin was copied from a fifth-century
> Athenian four-drachma coin.
>
> **Expansion.** The European Central Bank's record of the Greek national sides
> states the source of the design in plain words. The denomination changed, the
> metal changed, and the bird stayed.

**Source.** Existing site source `ecb` — European Central Bank, Greece: national
sides of euro coins. <https://www.ecb.europa.eu/euro/coins/html/gr.en.html>.
Verified wording: the owl in the design was copied from an ancient Athenian
4-drachma coin, fifth century BC.

**Caveats.** The ECB names a type, not a specimen — do not claim the euro design
copies any particular museum coin or any coin shown on this site. Keep the ECB's
own "copied from" rather than upgrading it into a claim about artistic method.

**Note.** The site already says this in the modern coda. For the creator kit it
needs no new source, only promotion.

### 4. Owls that nest in your purse

> **Hook.** In 414 BC Aristophanes had a chorus of birds promise the judges owls
> that would nest in their purses and hatch small change.
>
> **Expansion.** In *Birds* the chorus offers the competition judges a bribe: the
> owls of Laurion will never run out on them, and will move in and breed. The
> joke only works because the audience heard the coin and the bird in the same
> word.

**Source.** `hook-birds` — Aristophanes, *Birds* 1106–1108, Perseus Digital
Library, English translation Eugene O'Neill, Jr. (Random House, 1938).
<https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0026%3Acard%3D1102>
(Perseus paginates this as "lines 1102–1117"). Greek text at Perseus
`1999.01.0025`, same card.

**The exact translation.** O'Neill's published English promises that "the owls of
Laurium" will never be wanting, and has them building nests in the judges'
money-bags. The Greek at 1106–1108 reads:

> γλαῦκες ὑμᾶς οὔποτ᾽ ἐπιλείψουσι Λαυρειωτικαί·
> ἀλλ᾽ ἐνοικήσουσιν ἔνδον, ἔν τε τοῖς βαλλαντίοις
> ἐννεοττεύσουσι κἀκλέψουσι μικρὰ κέρματα.

**Caveats.** A comic bribe aimed at the play's judges, not a report of monetary
practice. The two versions differ at the punchline: the Greek verb at 1108 is
generally understood as *hatch out*, and the object is μικρὰ κέρματα, small
change, while O'Neill's English renders the line as the owls laying coins. Quote
the published translation or the Greek — do not paraphrase between them, and have
a Greek reader confirm any new rendering before it ships. O'Neill's 1938
translation may still be in copyright: link to Perseus and quote briefly rather
than reproducing the passage.

### 5. A design nobody wanted to improve

> **Hook.** Athens found a design that worked and then left it alone until it
> stopped striking tetradrachms altogether.
>
> **Expansion.** The American Numismatic Society's chief curator, writing about
> the first owls, notes that the type spelled out its own origin so completely
> that the Athenians saw little need to change the basic design until tetradrachm
> production ended in the first century BC. Style shifted over those centuries;
> the idea of the coin did not.

**Source.** `hook-ans-design` — Peter van Alfen, "The First Athenian Owls:
Symbols of What, Exactly?", ANS Pocket Change, 1 March 2021.
<https://numismatics.org/pocketchange/owls/>. The sentence about seeing little
need to change the basic design was confirmed in the page body, not in a search
summary.

**Caveats.** The claim is about the basic type, not the detail — helmet ornament,
eye rendering and style changed a great deal, which is what this site's coin
families are built on, so the hook must not flatten them. Van Alfen reports the
Peisistratid "branding" explanation as one current explanation ("the explanation
goes"), not as fact; keep it attributed and conditional. This is a curator's
public-facing post, not a peer-reviewed article.

### 6. A chisel cut, cataloged in four words

> **Hook.** Some surviving owls carry a chisel cut: one in the American
> Numismatic Society, found in Egypt, is catalogued simply as "One chisel cut."
>
> **Expansion.** An archaic Athenian tetradrachm from the Asyut hoard is recorded
> at 17.047 g and worn, with a single chisel cut noted in its condition record.
> Cutting was also official business: the Athenian law of 375/4 BC required the
> city's approver to cut through any coin with a bronze or lead core.

**Sources.** `hook-ans-testcut` — ANS 2008.39.34, Silver Tetradrachm, Athens,
515–482 BCE, from the Asyut hoard (IGCH 1644).
<http://numismatics.org/collection/2008.39.34>. Paired with existing source
`law`.

**Caveats.** The record states that this coin is cut. It does not say who cut it,
when, where or why. One catalogued specimen says nothing about how common test
cuts are — see "Could not verify", item 3. A cut is evidence to interpret; it is
never presented here as a way of telling a genuine coin from a false one, and
the site's existing glossary entry for "Test cut" already gets this right.

### 7. Silver that could have been shared out, turned into a fleet

> **Hook.** Athens once decided not to share out a windfall of mine silver, and
> built a fleet with it instead.
>
> **Expansion.** Herodotus says the revenues from the mines at Laurium were about
> to be divided at ten drachmae a man when Themistocles persuaded the Athenians
> to build two hundred ships for the war with Aegina. The *Athenaion Politeia*
> tells it differently: a hundred-talent profit from newly found mines at
> Maronea, lent to the hundred richest Athenians, a hundred triremes, and the
> battle of Salamis.

**Sources.** `hook-hdt-fleet` — Herodotus 7.144, translation A. D. Godley (1920),
Perseus.
<https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0126%3Abook%3D7%3Achapter%3D144>.
`hook-athpol-fleet` — *Athenaion Politeia* 22.7, translation H. Rackham (1952),
Perseus.
<https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0046%3Achapter%3D22>.

**Does the site already tell this?** No. Chapter 01's mining band covers Laurion,
the lease inscription and the 2025 isotope argument (`early-silver`, `mines`), but
the Themistocles episode is not on the site anywhere. It is new, and it is
supported.

**Caveats — and this is where the hook actually gets interesting.** The two
ancient accounts disagree on the mine, the number of ships and the mechanism.
Herodotus gives no year and ties the ships to the Aeginetan war, not to Salamis;
the *Athenaion Politeia* names Salamis. The conventional 483/2 BC comes from the
archon year, not from either text, and Rackham prints the archon as Nicomedes
where the archon of 483/2 is usually printed Nikodemos. Neither text mentions
owls, so the fleet must not be described as paid for in tetradrachms. Godley's
own footnote to 7.144 notes that ten drachmae a head would come to far too small
a sum to build two hundred ships, and reads the passage as a contribution to a
shipbuilding fund rather than the whole cost.

**Recommended framing.** Lead with the decision, not the battle, and let the
disagreement be the payoff — two ancient sources, two different stories, one
choice. That is more honest and more surprising than a tidy Salamis narrative.

### 8. 999 hoards

> **Hook.** A 2025 survey of 999 coin hoards found Athenian coinage pushing well
> past the Greek world, into the Levant and Egypt.
>
> **Expansion.** Zane Mullins assembled 999 hoards and 160,007 coins from 550 to
> 300 BC to measure how Classical Greek coinages actually moved. Among the
> results the study singles out is the penetration of Athenian coinage into
> non-Greek areas such as the Levant and Egypt, alongside a count of the foreign
> imitations that followed it.

**Source.** Existing site source `owl-circulation` — Zane Mullins, "The
circulation and distribution of classical Greek coinage", *The Economic History
Review*, 2025, doi:10.1111/ehr.70069. The abstract was read in full this session
and the figures are its own.

**What sources 29–31 already support.** `owl-circulation` (Mullins) carries the
hoard-based distribution across Egypt, the Levant, Mesopotamia, Iran and
Afghanistan; `owl-hoards` (Thompson 1979, Penn Museum) carries documented
geographic examples including Sicily and southwest Anatolia and the use of coins
as bullion; `iris-babylon` carries a single Athena–owl type attributed to Mazaces
and the mint of Babylon. Between them the geographic claim is already well
covered — what was missing was a quotable number, and Mullins supplies it.

**Caveats.** Hoards record where coins were buried, not routes travelled or how
they were used. The sample is 550–300 BC and is not a statement about every
period. Some hoard descriptions do not separate Athenian coins from imitations,
which the study handles as a category of its own.

**On "the dollar of the ancient world".** Flag it as journalistic. The phrase
"the dollar of the ancient Greek world" does appear — in the headline of an ANS
Pocket Change *video* post about the first owl (`hook-ans-dollar`,
4 March 2021) — but it is a headline analogy, not a claim argued in scholarly
text, and no opened scholarly source used it. If the site wants the punch, use
Mullins' numbers instead.

### 9. Eight days on a jury

> **Hook.** One owl was eight days on an Athenian jury.
>
> **Expansion.** The *Athenaion Politeia* lists what the city paid for public
> duties: half a drachma for the jury-courts, five obols for the Council, a
> drachma for an ordinary meeting of the Assembly. Four drachmas, the value of
> one tetradrachm, is eight days of jury pay.

**Source.** `hook-athpol-pay` — *Athenaion Politeia* 62.2, translation H. Rackham
(1952), Perseus.
<https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0046%3Achapter%3D62>.

**Caveats.** The text describes arrangements of its own time, the later fourth
century BC — not a rate that held for every year, and notably not the rate at the
time of the classical owls the site leads with. Its authorship is debated; it is
transmitted under Aristotle's name. Jury service was not daily employment, so
eight days of pay is not eight days of life. Rackham's translation may still be
in copyright: link rather than reproduce at length.

**A note on phrasing.** The familiar "three obols" for jurors comes from
Aristophanes; this text says "half a drachma", which is the same rate. Phrasing
the hook in drachmas avoids needing an obol conversion at all, and keeps the
arithmetic to one step.

### 10. Four days at sea

> **Hook.** For a rower in the fleet that sailed for Sicily in 415 BC, one owl
> was four days at sea.
>
> **Expansion.** Thucydides says the treasury gave a drachma a day to each seaman
> of that expedition, and that the trierarchs added bounties of their own on top.
> At four drachmas, one tetradrachm covers four days of the state's share.

**Source.** `hook-thuc-seamen` — Thucydides 6.31.3, translation Richard Crawley
(J. M. Dent, 1910), Perseus, CC BY-SA 3.0 US.
<https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0200%3Abook%3D6%3Achapter%3D31>.

**Caveats.** This is the rate for one expedition that Thucydides presents as
exceptionally costly — not a standing naval wage. Captains paid extra, so actual
earnings varied.

**Do not lead with Thucydides 3.17.** The hoplite pay at Potidaea (two drachmae a
day, one for the man and one for his servant) is attractive, but many editors
regard 3.17 as a later insertion and its figures as unreliable. It is recorded in
the JSON as `hook-thuc-hoplites` and held in reserve, not promoted.

## Also verified, ranked below the top ten

### 11. Six thousand talents on the Acropolis

> When the Peloponnesian War began, Pericles told Athens it had six thousand
> talents of coined silver sitting on the Acropolis — the value of about nine
> million tetradrachms.

Sources: `hook-thuc-reserve` (Thucydides 2.13.3, Crawley, Perseus) and
`hook-uc-money` (van Minnen, University of Cincinnati Classics) for the
accounting relation of 6,000 drachmas to the talent.

This is the safe substitute for "how many owls did Athens strike", which could
not be sourced at all. But note the two weaknesses: the figure sits inside a
speech Thucydides reports and is debated, and Thucydides says *coined silver*,
not tetradrachms — so nine million is an illustration of scale, never a mintage
figure or a count of owls that existed. It also needs two conversion steps, and
the conversion source is a teaching page rather than a museum. If the creator kit
wants only clean single-step facts, hold this one back.

### 12. The architect and the labourer were on the same wage

AIO's note 6 to *IG* I³ 476 states that the architect and the labourers, skilled
and unskilled alike, were on a one-drachma day-wage or on piece-rates computed
from it. One of the most refined buildings on the Acropolis, and a flat pay line.
Same source as hook 1, same caveat: this is AIO's summary of the rates behind the
accounts, covering one project in one year, and must not be generalised into a
claim about Athenian wage structure.

### 13. Somebody else's stamp

ANS 1949.128.4, a classical Athenian tetradrachm at 17.17 g, is recorded as
countermarked (`hook-ans-countermark`). Usable as supporting detail beside the
test-cut hook, not as a standalone shareable fact: the record flags the mark but
does not identify or locate the authority that made it, and no opened source
supported a general claim about Eastern countermarks as a class.

### 14. The dotted theta — keep what the site already says

The site's artifact story currently reads: ΑΘΕ abbreviates "of the Athenians";
the letters are alpha, theta and epsilon; on this coin theta is a circle with a
central dot, the ancient counterpart of the printed Θ. That is correct and
correctly sourced to `openlearn-theta`.

**The stronger hook is not supportable.** "The dot inside the theta is a
2,400-year-old letterform" implies a form still in use, and the dotted theta is
both older than the coin and superseded rather than carried forward into the
printed letter. No epigraphic authority on Attic letterforms could be opened for
this research beyond the Open University course resource already cited;
Wikipedia was consulted only as a pointer and is not cited. Recommendation: do
not promote this to the creator kit, and leave the existing wording alone.

## Could not verify

1. **Silver purity of owl tetradrachms** — e.g. "about 98% silver", or "over 75%
   of owls are at least 98% silver". The relevant study is Davis, Gore, Sheedy &
   Albarède, "Separating silver sources of Archaic Athenian coinage by
   comprehensive compositional analyses", *Journal of Archaeological Science* 114
   (2020) 105068, doi:10.1016/j.jas.2019.105068. Title, authors, journal and
   abstract were confirmed, and the abstract states that 788 coins were analysed
   by energy dispersive XRF — but it gives **no purity figure**. The open-access
   full text is hosted only on sciencedirect.com, which returned HTTP 403, and
   the HAL mirror returned a bot-protection page. **Do not publish any purity
   percentage**, including the claim that 86% of test-cut coins were over 98%
   silver: both figures appeared only in search-engine summaries. To resolve,
   read the body of that paper or its companion, "Studies in Athenian Silver
   Coinage: Analysis of Archaic 'Owl' Tetradrachms", *MiN* 6, 207–214.

2. **How many owls Athens struck**, in total or per year. No mintage estimate
   from a museum, the ANS or a peer-reviewed source could be located and opened.
   Die-study estimates exist in the specialist literature; none was read. Use
   hook 11 instead, which gives a sourced sense of magnitude without claiming a
   mintage.

3. **A frequency or proportion for test cuts** among surviving owls. Only dealer
   and hobbyist pages made quantitative claims. Do not publish "most owls are
   test cut" or any percentage. Hook 6 cites one specimen and the law, which is
   all that is supported.

4. **Eastern countermarks as a documented class**, with issuing authorities. One
   ANS record flagging a countermark was opened; no reputable source describing
   Eastern countermarking practice was. An ANS Pocket Change post that looked
   promising — van der Wilt, "Metrological Equivalencies: The Attic Tetradrachm
   in Persian Period Egypt", 8 August 2016 — turned out to be about weight
   standards and seigniorage, not countermarks. To resolve, consult van Alfen's
   published work on imitative owls or an ANS hoard study.

5. **"One of the most widely used and widely circulating coinages of the ancient
   Greek world."** This sentence is quoted all over the web and attributed to the
   ANS. Both candidate ANS pages were downloaded in full and searched directly:
   **the phrase is in neither body text.** It appears to originate in
   search-engine summarisation. Do not attribute it to the ANS or to van Alfen.
   What *is* verified is the headline phrase "the dollar of the ancient Greek
   world" on an ANS video post, recorded as `hook-ans-dollar` precisely so the
   provenance of the framing is on file.

6. **Two museum/teaching PDFs located but unreadable** — the Ashmolean Museum's
   "Ancient Greek Coins: Notes for Teachers" and the Attic Inscriptions Online
   A-level ancient history teachers' notes. Both downloaded; neither could be
   converted to text, as no PDF text-extraction tool is available in this
   environment. Nothing from either is used. The AIO notes are reported to state
   the obol/drachma/talent relations and would be a better citation than the
   University of Cincinnati teaching page used for hook 11 — worth opening in a
   reader and swapping in.

## Two standing cautions for whoever writes the creator kit

**The one-coin claim.** "Mint State after 2,400 years" is true of NGC
2086328-049 and of nothing else on this site. It must never appear in a list of
facts about owls in general, and the creator kit is exactly the place where that
slip would happen.

**Translation copyright.** Godley (Herodotus, 1920) and Crawley (Thucydides,
1910) are safe to quote; Perseus publishes the Crawley under CC BY-SA 3.0 US.
Rackham (*Athenaion Politeia*, 1952) and O'Neill (*Birds*, 1938) may still be in
copyright — link to Perseus and keep quotations to a phrase.
