# A fictional life, with historical parallels

Updated 17 September 2026. The owner requested an engaging fictional account of
how an owl could survive with little wear, including gifts and a royal collector,
and an explanation of the history of collecting.

“The gift that waited” is a four-scene interlude on `/one-owl/`. Its family,
dialogue, hidden savings, rediscovery, scholar and unnamed king are invented.
Visible labels introduce every scene and mark the beginning and end of the
fiction. No named historical collector is linked to NGC 2086328-049. The source
notes belong to the factual discussion after the story, not to its invented
events. This is an explicit, requested exception to the factual narrative;
the main atlas and the specimen’s documented modern record are unchanged.

## Historical basis

- Milne’s 1933 *The Beni Hasan Coin-Hoard*, pp. 119–121, reports 54 Athenian-type
  tetradrachms with exceptional preservation and little apparent circulation.
  The opening primary paragraph was available in an indexed scan; retrieval of
  the full page image failed. The page restricts its claim to that paragraph.
  His terminology is not a modern grade, and his article discusses an Egyptian
  origin for the pieces. These are not identified as the photographed coin.
- The existing Acropolis Museum hoard record and Canadian Conservation Institute
  guidance support a possible long interval out of circulation and the caveat
  that burial may also corrode silver. There is no numerical claim that most
  high-grade owls survived through any one route.
- Suetonius, *Augustus* 75, describes old royal and foreign coins as gifts.
  This demonstrates appreciation and gifting, not proof of a modern hobby.
- The Smithsonian’s *Collecting Money* associates the recognizable hobby with
  the Renaissance while noting possible Roman precedents. Its institutional
  text was available through the search index; direct retrieval returned 500.
- Petrarch’s *Familiar Letters* XIX.3 describes giving treasured Roman coins to
  Charles IV. The 1914 Robinson/Rolfe translation, pp. 370–376, was read in full
  at Project Gutenberg. The page uses “fourteenth century” because the encounter
  and the letter have different dates.
- The BnF’s own departmental history describes Louis XIV expanding the royal
  Cabinet and bringing it to Versailles for daily study. This supports the
  royal-collector setting without asserting that he owned this owl.

Exact links, access dates and qualifications are in `src/one-owl.json` and appear
in the companion bibliography. Four new references supplement the earlier eight.
No photographs were added or changed. The four owner photographs remain original
files with their existing credits and scoped permission notices.

## Presentation

The story uses the existing paper palette, editorial typography and natural
scrolling. A desktop side note stays alongside the scenes; on mobile the same
fiction explanation precedes them. The collecting history becomes a vertical
sequence on small screens. All text remains available without JavaScript.
