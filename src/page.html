01 / SILVER FROM LAURION

First the silver.
Then the owl.

Athens is a city in Attica, and Attica held Laurion — the silver-mining district behind the city’s coinage. {{CITE:attica,mines}}

An inscribed fourth-century lease record names mines, boundaries, workshops and a furnace: the owl belonged to an economic landscape, not only to an engraver’s bench. A 2025 isotope study argues against a neat “new silver, therefore new design” story. {{CITE:mines,early-silver}}

What Athens struck from it was the tetradrachm, worth four drachmas, and it was used far beyond Attica. {{CITE:met,acropolis,owl-circulation}}

FROM SILVER TO A COIN

How do you
make an owl?

The pictures began in the tools. Two engraved dies pressed a design into each side of a silver blank. {{CITE:met-minting}}

  1. 01 / THE TOOLS

    Engrave the dies.

    A die is a metal tool with an engraved design. The lower die makes Athena; the upper die makes her owl.

  2. 02 / THE SILVER

    Place the blank.

    The blank, called a flan, sits on the lower die fixed in the anvil. The upper die goes above it.

  3. 03 / THE STRIKE

    Strike the upper die.

    A hammer blow drives the silver against both engraved surfaces, impressing the two faces of the coin together.

Schematic side views. Tool shapes, engraved marks and spacing are simplified; not to scale. Silver is highlighted in gold. Where it runs, the optional animation shows the order of the steps, not a measured speed, force or number of blows, and the struck disc carries no design.

02 / THE CLASSICAL MASS ISSUES

An owl becomes
an icon.

Athens struck heraldic coins, the Wappenmünzen, before it adopted Athena and her owl. When the city changed, and why, remain research questions. {{CITE:early-silver}}

03 / THE END OF THE PELOPONNESIAN WAR
404 BCE

The war ends.
The story does not.

In 404 BCE, Athens surrendered to Sparta. The Peloponnesian War ended, marking a rupture in the history of the city and its coinage. {{CITE:ngc}}

This is the conventional endpoint of the great late-fifth-century mass issue. Older coins continued to circulate, and Athenian coinage continued into the fourth century. {{CITE:ngc,profile}}

05 / PARALLEL TRADITIONS

Not every owl
came from Athens.

In the fourth century BCE, alongside the changes in Athens, other authorities were making owls of their own.

Egypt offers a documented example.

The Athena–owl issues attributed to Sabakes belong to Persian-administered Egypt, around 340–333 BCE. IRIS catalogues them as an Egyptian type. {{CITE:sabakes}}

Local inscriptions on some southern Levantine issues offer another clue to their issuing communities. Other attributions are disputed. {{CITE:isotopes}}

Here, “imitation” means adapting another issuer’s design. Such ancient local coinages are a different category from plated counterfeits or modern forgeries. {{CITE:law,sabakes}}

{{PHOTO:sabakes:foreign-image}} EGYPT / SABAKES

A different authority.

Where a coin was made, where it travelled and where it was found are three different claims. Each close-up below shows one area; the locator beside it keeps that area in the wider world.

WHERE IT WAS MADE

Mint.

The authority and place that issued a coin. An owl design alone does not prove an Athenian origin. {{CITE:sabakes,isotopes}}

WHERE IT TRAVELLED

Circulation.

Coins could move as money or as silver valued by weight. The same weight standard could also be used by different issuers. {{CITE:owl-hoards,met,cma-classic,sabakes}}

WHERE IT WAS FOUND

Findspot.

A hoard is a group of coins deposited together. Its location records one point in those coins’ histories, not every stop or a complete trade route. {{CITE:owl-circulation,owl-hoards}}

06 / THE COIN IS EVIDENCE, NOT THE WHOLE ANSWER

Look closer.
Follow the evidence.

I.

Style

Eye, helmet, lettering, owl, flan. No single feature decides. {{CITE:profile}}

II.

Context

An excavation records a deposit; a market coin may have none. {{CITE:isotopes}}

III.

Metal

Lead isotopes can narrow a silver source, never settle it. {{CITE:early-silver}}

IV.

Documents

Laws, leases and accounts record how the money was handled. {{CITE:law,mines,accounts}}

375/4
ONE DOCUMENT, READ CLOSELY

A fake owl, cut through and given to a goddess.

Nikophon’s law of 375/4 BCE put a public approver among the bankers’ tables in the Agora. A coin with a bronze or lead core was to be cut through at once, and then became sacred property of the Mother of the Gods, deposited with the Council. A separate clause on foreign owl-type silver survives only in a debated restoration. {{CITE:law}}
