01 / THE CITY AND ITS SILVER

The city behind
the owl.

REGION ATTICA
ISSUING CITY Athens The city named by ΑΘΕ

Region and city: a schematic relationship, not a geographical map.

Athens gave the owl its civic identity. Attica, the region surrounding the city, places that identity in a landscape. [05] [04]

The coin names a community, not just a place. On the reverse, ΑΘΕ abbreviates “of the Athenians.” Geography and inscription answer different parts of the same question: whose money is this? [05] [04]

“Attic standard” means something else: a weight convention. The illustrated Cleveland tetradrachm weighs 17.2 grams. Neither an Attic-style weight nor an owl automatically proves that a coin was struck in Athens. [03] [13]

BEFORE THE COIN, THE SILVER

The story begins below ground.

The Laurion mining district lay in southeastern Attica. An inscribed fourth-century lease record preserves named mines, property boundaries, workshops and a furnace: the owl belonged to an economic landscape, not just an engraver’s bench. [06] [11]

A 2025 isotope study argues against a neat “new silver discovery, therefore new owl design” origin story. The transition in metal supply appears more complex than that. [06]

A useful family tree.
Not a set of clean borders.

Athens struck earlier money commonly called Wappenmünzen , or “heraldic coins,” before the Athena–owl type. The precise timing and explanation of the change remain research questions. [06]

Collectors sometimes call refined fifth-century owls “transitional.” The same word also appears around early fourth-century issues. Always ask: transitional between which groups, according to which reference? [21] [08]

03 / THE CLASSICAL MASS ISSUES

An owl becomes
an icon.

By the mid-fifth century BCE, Athens was producing the great classical mass issues: the compact Athena–owl design at the heart of this story. [21]

These silver tetradrachms circulated beyond the city. The recurring design made Athenian identity conspicuous even as individual dies, strikes and flans differed. [01] [02]

Recognition lives in the details: Athena’s helmet and eye on one face; the owl, olive, crescent and civic initials on the other. [01] [04]

04 / THE END OF THE PELOPONNESIAN WAR
404 BCE

The war ends.
The story does not.

In 404 BCE, Athens surrendered to Sparta. The Peloponnesian War ended, marking a rupture in the history of the city and its coinage. [21]

Numismatists conventionally end the great late-fifth-century mass issue here. That boundary does not mean that every owl disappeared, that Athenian coinage ended, or that coins already struck stopped circulating. [21] [08]

A TURNING POINT, NOT AN END

Fourth-century issues retain the Athena–owl pairing while changing the treatment of the eye and helmet. The next chapter follows the design through a changed Athens. [08] [07]

PI-STYLE / MID–LATE FOURTH CENTURY BCE

One ornament.
A new name.

“Pi-style” takes its name from a developed helmet ornament that suggests the Greek letter π. Folded, restruck flans are important to the group’s interpretation. The photograph is a real, catalogue-identified Pi III specimen—not a reconstruction of the ornament. [07] [22]

THIS SPECIMEN’S CATALOGUE DATEc. 353–295 BCE

BnF’s dating convention for this record. Not an exact year of striking, and not a settled boundary for all Pi-style coinage. [22]

A CHRONOLOGY WITH FOOTNOTES

Use c. 165/164 BCE into the first century BCE as an orientation, not an exact lifespan. The familiar 42 BCE terminal date is not universal: another catalog tradition ends at 63/62. Specialist chronology has been revised repeatedly. [20] [14]

07 / A FAMILY TREE WITH BRANCHES

Not every owl
came from Athens.

Imitation is part of the history. It is not automatically an accusation.

A familiar design can travel farther than its issuing authority.

For a concrete example, look at the Athena–owl issues attributed to Sabakes in Persian-administered Egypt, around 340–333 BCE. IRIS catalogs these as an Egyptian type, not an Athenian style period. [13]

Other owl-type coinages in the southern Levant used local inscriptions and design choices. But a coin found there is not necessarily local, and an unusual-looking owl is not automatically an imitation. [09]

Keep ancient local issues, ancient plated counterfeits, and modern forgeries in separate conceptual boxes. They raise different historical questions; “it is not Athenian” does not mean “it is not ancient.” [10] [13]

08 / THE COIN IS EVIDENCE, NOT THE WHOLE ANSWER

Look closer.
Follow the evidence.

I.

Style

Eye, helmet, lettering, owl, flan. A comparison starts with details; no single feature carries every conclusion. [08]

II.

Context

A controlled excavation gives evidence a market provenance cannot automatically replace. Findspot and mint remain separate. [09]

III.

Metal

Lead-isotope measurements can constrain a silver-source argument. They need interpretation alongside the other evidence. [06]

IV.

Documents

Laws, leases and treasury accounts show how people administered and used the money. [10] [11] [12]

2026 THE STORY IS STILL BEING REVISED

What if an “imitation”
was Athenian all along?

A new isotope study argues that several unusual late-fifth-century owls from excavated sites in the southern Levant fit Attic or Athenian-controlled metal sources. Its authors reassess their attribution and advocate a 404 BCE endpoint for the studied late-fifth-century series. [09]

The conclusions concern the sampled coins and their proposed metal sources. They do not settle the origin of every eastern owl or authenticate coins outside the study. [09]

REFERENCE / OPTIONAL Understanding coin descriptions +

Museum and catalogue descriptions bring together place, denomination and attribution. Each answers a different question; none replaces a close look at the object.

Place & issuing authority
“Attica, Athens” identifies a region and an issuing city. A findspot records where a coin was discovered, not necessarily where it was made. [05] [09]
Metal & denomination
AR identifies silver. A tetradrachm is a four-drachma denomination; its recorded weight and diameter describe the particular specimen. [01]
Date & attribution
A date range places an issue within a proposed chronology; it does not identify the exact year in which every specimen was struck. Museum object dates and broader stylistic periods can differ. [01] [21]

A small language for small objects.

THE GLOSSARY
Attica

The region surrounding Athens. “Attica, Athens” is region plus issuing city, not two separate owl varieties. [05]

Attic standard

A weight convention used beyond Athens. The illustrated Cleveland tetradrachm weighs 17.2 g; findspot, weight standard and mint are separate questions. [03] [13]

Tetradrachm

A denomination of four drachmas. AR in a catalog identifies silver, not a place or a mint mark. [01]

Obverse / reverse

The two faces of a coin. For these Athenian types, the Athena face is conventionally the obverse and the owl face the reverse. [01]

Flan

The metal blank that receives the design. Its shape and folded or restruck fabric may preserve evidence of manufacture. [07]

Incuse

A recessed area. On many older owl reverses, the design lies within an incuse square. [04]

Ethnic

An inscription naming a people or civic community; ΑΘΕ is the abbreviated ethnic of the Athenians. [04]

Pi-style

A modern stylistic label referring to the helmet ornament—not the value, weight, or mathematical properties of the coin. [07]

Imitation

A coin using another issuer’s design language. An ancient imitation can be real silver and historically legitimate; this is not the same category as a modern forgery. [13] [10]

Test cut

A deliberate incision used to investigate metal beneath the surface. A cut is evidence to interpret, not proof of a precise route or an automatic guarantee of authenticity. [10] [09]

Hoard / findspot

A hoard is a deposited group of objects; a findspot records discovery. Neither is automatically the place where a coin was minted. [09]

c. / BCE

Circa means approximately. BCE uses the same numbered years as BC: 440 BCE and 440 BC refer to the same year.

BnF photographs: public-domain assertions and reuse review

The individual Commons file records for Pi III (BnF 1475), Pi II (BnF 1469), and quadridigité (BnF 1478) assert public-domain status in France under PD-France. This atlas records that assertion as such—not as a CC0 grant or as direct permission from BnF.

BnF separately publishes conditions for commercial and promotional reuse, including stated exceptions. The project-specific application remains unresolved. All six photographs therefore carry a visible Reuse review pending notice in their captions, viewer and exported metadata. No permission request has been sent. [28]

Institutional identification, image resolution and reuse status are separate questions. The Pi-style and quadridigité exhibits have documented museum specimens; that identification does not itself establish publication clearance.

Reading the citations and dating conventions

Museum records support the identity and metadata of the illustrated objects; research papers support wider arguments. Older catalog descriptions are preserved in image notes even when this edition does not adopt their chronology. Entries explicitly identify bibliographic references whose full text has not been independently rechecked.

A slash in 375/4 BCE refers to a year spanning parts of two modern calendar years, rather than uncertainty between unrelated centuries. Broad stylistic envelopes overlap. “Approximately” does not mean a dated historical event itself is uncertain.

Image delivery, privacy and long-term preservation

This portable edition loads images from their museum or Wikimedia hosts when you open it online. Those requests disclose ordinary browser connection information to those hosts. There are no analytics, trackers, external fonts, cookies or accounts added by this site. Your motion preference is stored locally in your browser when storage is available.

Remote files can move or become unavailable. The source package includes an image manifest and an optional vendoring script to download permitted photographs into your own deployment; a publishable reference should maintain those local copies with their credits and licenses. These downloads were not completed in the build environment.
