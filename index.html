01 / WHERE THE OWLS WERE MADE & USED

From Athens
to a wider world.

The owl began as the money of Athens, an ancient Greek city-state. Its reach extended beyond the city that struck it. [04] [29]

Start with Athens and its surrounding region, Attica. Then explore the islands, countries and historical regions connected with owl coinage. Each close-up shows an area; the small locator keeps it in the wider world.

01 / City · region · country

Athens & Attica

The modern region helps locate Attica; its borders are not a reconstruction of the ancient city-state.Each view has its own approximate scale. North is always up.

Begin with a city and its countryside.

Athens is a city in Attica, the surrounding region on the Greek mainland. Both are in present-day Greece.

The starting point: the Aegean world.

The Athenian mint

Athens issued silver tetradrachms—coins worth four drachmas. Attica also contained the Laurion silver-mining district, introduced below. [05] [04] [01] [11]

West, across the sea.

Sicily is the large island just off the “toe” of Italy. It lies west of Greece, in the central Mediterranean.

West of Athens.

Evidence of circulation

Athenian owls occur in Sicilian hoards. These finds document coins reaching the island; they do not make Sicily an Athenian mint. [30]

03 / Peninsula

Anatolia

The outline is for orientation; the ancient region and its political divisions changed over time.Each view has its own approximate scale. North is always up.

Across the Aegean, into Anatolia.

Anatolia is the large peninsula east of the Aegean Sea, forming the Asian part of present-day Türkiye. Greece faces it across the water.

East of Athens, across the Aegean.

Evidence of circulation

Hoard evidence records Athenian coins in southwest Anatolia. That regional evidence does not establish a route through every part of the peninsula. [30] [29]

04 / Country · Nile valley

Egypt

The modern country outline locates Egypt. It is not a map of a particular ancient kingdom or the distribution of coin finds.Each view has its own approximate scale. North is always up.

South to the Nile.

Egypt lies on the southern shore of the Mediterranean. The Nile runs north through it to a broad delta on the coast.

South of Athens, across the Mediterranean.

Circulation and related local issues

Egyptian hoards include Athenian coins, sometimes valued as silver by weight. Later, authorities in Persian-administered Egypt issued related owl-type coins, including the Sabakes type shown in chapter 07. [30] [13]

05 / Historical region

The Levant

The hatched countries locate the broad region. They do not define a single ancient state or an exact historical boundary.Each view has its own approximate scale. North is always up.

Along the Mediterranean’s eastern edge.

The Levant is a regional name for the eastern Mediterranean lands. It includes areas now in Syria, Lebanon, Israel, Palestine and Jordan; its extent varies with context.

Southeast of Anatolia; northeast of Egypt.

Circulation and debated local issues

Athenian coins reached the Levant, and related owl-type issues also circulated there. Some coins once called eastern imitations have been reassessed as Athenian; a findspot alone cannot settle the mint. [29] [09]

Babylonia was in southern Mesopotamia, in what is now southern Iraq. The Tigris and Euphrates cross this lowland toward the Persian Gulf. Babylon was a city within it.

Iran lies east of Iraq. Afghanistan lies farther east, beyond Iran. These are two distinct countries, far inland from the Aegean starting point.

Circulation.

Coins could move as money or as silver valued by weight. The same weight standard could also be used by different issuers. [30] [01] [03] [13]

Findspot.

A hoard is a group of coins deposited together. Its location records one point in those coins’ histories, not every stop or a complete trade route. [29] [30]

BEFORE THE COIN, THE SILVER

The story begins below ground.

The Laurion mining district lay in southeastern Attica. An inscribed fourth-century lease record preserves named mines, property boundaries, workshops and a furnace: the owl belonged to an economic landscape, not just an engraver’s bench. [06] [11]

A 2025 isotope study argues against a neat “new silver discovery, therefore new owl design” origin story. The transition in metal supply appears more complex than that. [06]

FROM SILVER TO A COIN

How do you
make an owl?

The pictures began in the tools. Two engraved dies pressed a design into each side of a silver blank. [32]

  1. 01 / THE TOOLS

    Engrave the dies.

    A die is a metal tool with an engraved design. The lower die makes Athena; the upper die makes her owl.

  2. 02 / THE SILVER

    Place the blank.

    The blank, called a flan, sits on the lower die fixed in the anvil. The upper die goes above it.

  3. 03 / THE STRIKE

    Strike the upper die.

    A hammer blow drives the silver against both engraved surfaces, impressing the two faces of the coin together.

Schematic side views. Tool shapes, engraved marks and spacing are simplified; not to scale. Silver is highlighted in gold.

03 / THE CLASSICAL MASS ISSUES

An owl becomes
an icon.

By the mid-fifth century BCE, Athens was producing the great classical mass issues: the compact Athena–owl design at the heart of this story. [21]

Individual dies, strikes and flans differ, but the pairing of Athena and her owl gives the series a recognizable civic identity. The details below show how to read that design. [01] [02]

Athena comes first.

On the obverse, Athena faces right in a crested helmet. Begin with the whole portrait before looking at the engraver’s smaller choices. [03]

02 / Athena’s eye

The face turns. The eye does not quite follow.

The large almond-shaped eye appears almost frontal within the profile face. Keep it in mind: later owls give Athena a more fully profile eye. [03] [08]

03 / The helmet

A helmet worth remembering.

The helmet’s ornament is part of the design’s identity. Its later changes help distinguish the Pi-style family you will meet farther down the story. [07]

04 / The owl

Turn the coin. Meet the owl.

Its body turns right, but its head faces us. The bird stands within a recessed, or incuse, square formed in striking. [04]

05 / The olive sprig

A branch beside the bird.

An olive sprig fills the space beside the owl. Even on this small surface, the engraver makes room for individual leaves. [04] [02]

06 / The crescent

A small crescent. An open question.

A crescent accompanies the olive sprig. Its presence is clear; a precise commemorative meaning should not be assumed. [02]

07 / ΑΘΕ

Three letters. A civic signature.

ΑΘΕ abbreviates “of the Athenians.” The letters are alpha, theta and epsilon. On this coin, theta is a circle with a central dot—the ancient counterpart of the printed Θ. [04] [41]

All of this, on a piece of money.

04 / THE END OF THE PELOPONNESIAN WAR
404 BCE

The war ends.
The story does not.

In 404 BCE, Athens surrendered to Sparta. The Peloponnesian War ended, marking a rupture in the history of the city and its coinage. [21]

This is the conventional endpoint of the great late-fifth-century mass issue. Older coins continued to circulate, and Athenian coinage continued into the fourth century. [21] [08]

A CHRONOLOGY WITH FOOTNOTES

Use c. 165/164 BCE into the first century BCE as an orientation, not an exact lifespan. The familiar 42 BCE terminal date is not universal: another catalog tradition ends at 63/62. Specialist chronology has been revised repeatedly. [20] [14]

07 / PARALLEL TRADITIONS

Not every owl
came from Athens.

In the fourth century BCE, alongside the changes in Athens, other authorities were making owls of their own.

Egypt offers a documented example.

The Athena–owl issues attributed to Sabakes belong to Persian-administered Egypt, around 340–333 BCE. IRIS catalogues them as an Egyptian type. [13]

Local inscriptions on some southern Levantine issues offer another clue to their issuing communities. Other attributions are disputed, as the research in the next section shows. [09]

Here, “imitation” means adapting another issuer’s design. Such ancient local coinages are a different category from plated counterfeits or modern forgeries. [10] [13]

08 / THE COIN IS EVIDENCE, NOT THE WHOLE ANSWER

Look closer.
Follow the evidence.

Resolving an attribution brings several kinds of evidence together.

I.

Style

Eye, helmet, lettering, owl, flan. A comparison starts with details; no single feature carries every conclusion. [08]

II.

Context

A controlled excavation records the deposit and associated objects. That context may be missing for coins known only through the market. [09]

III.

Metal

Lead-isotope measurements can constrain a silver-source argument. They need interpretation alongside the other evidence. [06]

IV.

Documents

Laws, leases and treasury accounts show how people administered and used the money. [10] [11] [12]

2026 THE STORY IS STILL BEING REVISED

What if an “imitation”
was Athenian all along?

A new isotope study argues that several unusual late-fifth-century owls from excavated sites in the southern Levant fit Attic or Athenian-controlled metal sources. Its authors reassess their attribution and advocate a 404 BCE endpoint for the studied late-fifth-century series. [09]

The conclusions concern the sampled coins and their proposed metal sources. They do not settle the origin of every eastern owl or authenticate coins outside the study. [09]

REFERENCE / OPTIONAL Understanding coin descriptions +

Museum and catalogue descriptions bring together place, denomination and attribution. Each answers a different question; none replaces a close look at the object.

Place & issuing authority
“Attica, Athens” identifies a region and an issuing city. A findspot records where a coin was discovered, not necessarily where it was made. [05] [09]
Metal & denomination
AR identifies silver. A tetradrachm is a four-drachma denomination; its recorded weight and diameter describe the particular specimen. [01]
Date & attribution
A date range places an issue within a proposed chronology; it does not identify the exact year in which every specimen was struck. Museum object dates and broader stylistic periods can differ. [01] [21]

A small language for small objects.

THE GLOSSARY
Attica

The region around Athens in present-day Greece. Athens was the issuing city-state. “Attica, Athens” identifies region and city, not two separate owl varieties. [05]

Attic standard

A weight convention used beyond Athens. The illustrated Cleveland tetradrachm weighs 17.2 g; findspot, weight standard and mint are separate questions. [03] [13]

Tetradrachm

A denomination of four drachmas. AR in a catalog identifies silver, not a place or a mint mark. [01]

Obverse / reverse

The two faces of a coin. For these Athenian types, the Athena face is conventionally the obverse and the owl face the reverse. [01]

Flan

The metal blank that receives the design. Its shape and folded or restruck fabric may preserve evidence of manufacture. [07]

Incuse

A recessed area. On many older owl reverses, the design lies within an incuse square. [04]

Ethnic

An inscription naming a people or civic community; ΑΘΕ is the abbreviated ethnic of the Athenians. [04]

Pi-style

A modern stylistic label referring to the helmet ornament—not the value, weight, or mathematical properties of the coin. [07]

Imitation

A coin using another issuer’s design language. An ancient imitation can be real silver and historically legitimate; this is not the same category as a modern forgery. [13] [10]

Test cut

A deliberate incision used to investigate metal beneath the surface. A cut is evidence to interpret, not proof of a precise route or an automatic guarantee of authenticity. [10] [09]

Hoard / findspot

A hoard is a deposited group of objects; a findspot records discovery. Neither is automatically the place where a coin was minted. [09]

c. / BCE

Circa means approximately. BCE uses the same numbered years as BC: 440 BCE and 440 BC refer to the same year.

BnF photographs: public-domain assertions and reuse review

The individual Commons file records for Pi III (BnF 1475), Pi II (BnF 1469), and quadridigité (BnF 1478) assert public-domain status in France under PD-France. This atlas records that assertion as such—not as a CC0 grant or as direct permission from BnF.

BnF separately publishes conditions for commercial and promotional reuse, including stated exceptions. The project-specific application remains unresolved. All six photographs therefore carry a visible Reuse review pending notice in their captions, viewer and exported metadata. No permission request has been sent. [28]

Institutional identification, image resolution and reuse status are separate questions. The Pi-style and quadridigité exhibits have documented museum specimens; that identification does not itself establish publication clearance.

Reading the citations and dating conventions

Museum records support the identity and metadata of the illustrated objects; research papers support wider arguments. Older catalog descriptions are preserved in image notes even when this edition does not adopt their chronology. Entries explicitly identify bibliographic references whose full text has not been independently rechecked.

A slash in 375/4 BCE refers to a year spanning parts of two modern calendar years, rather than uncertainty between unrelated centuries. Broad stylistic envelopes overlap. “Approximately” does not mean a dated historical event itself is uncertain.

Image delivery, privacy and long-term preservation

This portable edition loads images from their museum or Wikimedia hosts when you open it online. Those requests disclose ordinary browser connection information to those hosts. There are no analytics, trackers, external fonts, cookies or accounts added by this site. Your motion preference is stored locally in your browser when storage is available.

Remote files can move or become unavailable. The source package includes an image manifest and an optional vendoring script to download permitted photographs into your own deployment; a publishable reference should maintain those local copies with their credits and licenses. These downloads were not completed in the build environment.
