02 / THE FACTS

Say these
things.

Ten facts about the owl, each one traced to a document somebody actually opened. The first line is the post. The paragraph under it is what you say when the comments ask. The collapsed note is what stops you being wrong — read it before you press publish.

TWO STANDING WARNINGS

“Mint State after 2,400 years” is about one coin. It is true of NGC 2086328-049, the single certified tetradrachm in One Owl, and of nothing else on this site. It is not a fact about Athenian owls as a class, and most surviving owls are nothing like it. Do not put it in a list of owl facts.

Some translations are still in copyright. Godley’s Herodotus (1920) and Crawley’s Thucydides (1910) are safe to quote. Rackham’s Athenian Constitution (1952) and O’Neill’s Birds (1938) may not be — link to Perseus and keep any quotation to a phrase rather than reproducing the passage.

  1. 01PURCHASING POWER

    On the Acropolis in 408/7 BC, one owl was about four days’ pay.

    The building accounts for the Erechtheion record the wages of the people finishing the temple, and Attic Inscriptions Online summarises the rate they worked to: a day-wage of one drachma, or piece-rates worked out from it. A tetradrachm was four drachmas, so a single owl stood roughly for a four-day week of that work. {{CITE:hook-erechtheion,met}}

    Before you post this
    • Most surviving entries in the accounts are piece-rates for named tasks, not counted days; the one-drachma day-wage is AIO’s summary of how the rates were computed, not a single quoted line.
    • The accounts cover one year of finishing work at one building, in the last years of the Peloponnesian War. They are not a wage series for the fifth century.
    • Nothing says a worker was actually handed a tetradrachm. The equivalence is arithmetic on the denomination, not a recorded payment.
  2. 02COUNTERFEITS

    An Athenian law of 375/4 BC ordered that a fake owl be cut open on the spot and handed over to a goddess.

    Nikophon’s law put a public approver among the bankers’ tables in the Agora and by the stele of Poseidon in Piraeus, and told him what to do with a coin that was not what it claimed. A piece with a bronze or lead core was to be cut through at once, and then became sacred property of the Mother of the Gods, deposited with the Council. {{CITE:law}}

    Before you post this
    • The law is a document of 375/4 BC. It does not describe practice in every earlier or later year.
    • The clause on foreign silver struck with an Athenian type is partly restored; AIO records the restoration as plausible but notes the missing parallels. Do not build a claim on that clause.
    • This is a rule about what officials did, not a method a reader can use to judge a coin today.
  3. 03THE MODERN ECHO

    The owl on the Greek one-euro coin was copied from a fifth-century Athenian four-drachma coin.

    The European Central Bank’s record of the Greek national sides states the source of the design in plain words. The denomination changed, the metal changed, and the bird stayed. {{CITE:ecb}}

    Before you post this
    • The ECB names a type, not a particular specimen. Do not claim the euro design copies any single museum coin, or any coin shown on this site.
    • “Copied from” is the ECB’s own wording; keep it rather than upgrading it to a claim about artistic method.
  4. 04OWLS IN COMEDY

    In 414 BC Aristophanes had a chorus of birds promise the judges owls that would nest in their purses and hatch small change.

    In Birds the chorus offers the competition judges a bribe: the owls of Laurion will never run out on them, and will move in and breed. The joke only works because the audience heard the coin and the bird in the same word. {{CITE:hook-birds}}

    Before you post this
    • This is a comic bribe aimed at the play’s judges, not a report of monetary practice.
    • The Greek at line 1108 has the owls hatching out small change (μικρὰ κέρματα); Eugene O’Neill’s published English renders this as laying coins. Quote the published translation, or the Greek, rather than paraphrasing between them.
    • O’Neill’s 1938 translation may still be in copyright. Link to Perseus and quote briefly; do not reproduce the passage at length.
  5. 05A DELIBERATELY OLD-FASHIONED COIN

    Athens found a design that worked and then left it alone until it stopped striking tetradrachms altogether.

    The American Numismatic Society’s curator, writing about the first owls, notes that the type spelled out its own origin so completely that the Athenians saw little need to change the basic design until tetradrachm production ended in the first century BC. Style shifted over those centuries; the idea of the coin did not. {{CITE:hook-ans-design}}

    Before you post this
    • The claim is about the basic type, not about detail. Helmet ornament, eye rendering and style changed a great deal, which is what the coin families on this site are built on.
    • The explanation that the Peisistratids chose the type as a “brand” for exported silver is reported by van Alfen as a current explanation, not asserted as fact. Keep it attributed and conditional.
    • This is a curator’s public-facing post, not a peer-reviewed article.
  6. 06TEST CUTS

    Some surviving owls carry a chisel cut: one in the American Numismatic Society, found in Egypt, is catalogued simply as “One chisel cut”.

    An archaic Athenian tetradrachm from the Asyut hoard is recorded at 17.047 g and worn, with a single chisel cut noted in its condition record. Cutting was also official business: the Athenian law of 375/4 BC required the city’s approver to cut through any coin with a bronze or lead core. {{CITE:hook-ans-testcut,law}}

    Before you post this
    • The ANS record states that this coin is cut. It does not say who cut it, when, where or why.
    • One catalogued specimen is not evidence for how common test cuts are. No reliable overall proportion could be verified, so do not post a percentage.
    • A cut is evidence to interpret. Do not present it as a way of telling a genuine coin from a false one.
  7. 07SILVER INTO SHIPS

    Athens once decided not to share out a windfall of mine silver, and built a fleet with it instead.

    Herodotus says the revenues from the mines at Laurium were about to be divided at ten drachmae a man when Themistocles persuaded the Athenians to build two hundred ships for the war with Aegina. The Athenaion Politeia tells it differently: a hundred-talent profit from newly found mines at Maronea, lent to the hundred richest Athenians, a hundred triremes, and the battle of Salamis. {{CITE:hook-hdt-fleet,hook-athpol-fleet}}

    Before you post this
    • The two ancient accounts disagree on the mine, the number of ships and the mechanism. Present the disagreement rather than merging them — it is the better story anyway.
    • Herodotus does not date the episode and does not connect these ships to Salamis; the Athenaion Politeia does. The conventional 483/2 BC comes from the archon year, not from either text.
    • Neither text mentions owls. Do not say the fleet was paid for in tetradrachms.
    • Godley’s own footnote observes that ten drachmae a head would come to far too small a sum to build 200 ships, and reads the passage as a contribution to a shipbuilding fund.
  8. 08REACH

    A 2025 survey of 999 coin hoards found Athenian coinage pushing well past the Greek world, into the Levant and Egypt.

    Zane Mullins assembled 999 hoards and 160,007 coins from 550 to 300 BC to measure how Classical Greek coinages actually moved. Among the results the study singles out is the penetration of Athenian coinage into non-Greek areas such as the Levant and Egypt, alongside a count of the foreign imitations that followed it. {{CITE:owl-circulation,hook-ans-dollar}}

    Before you post this
    • Hoards record where coins were buried, not the routes they travelled or how they were used.
    • The sample covers 550–300 BC. It is not a statement about every period.
    • Some hoard descriptions do not separate Athenian coins from imitations; the study discusses imitations as a category of its own.
    • “The dollar of the ancient world” is popular framing, not a research finding. It appears as the headline of an ANS outreach video post about the first owl, and no scholarly source we opened used it. Mullins’ numbers do the same job honestly.
  9. 09PURCHASING POWER

    One owl was eight days on an Athenian jury.

    The Athenaion Politeia lists what the city paid for public duties: half a drachma for the jury-courts, five obols for the Council, a drachma for an ordinary meeting of the Assembly. Four drachmas, the value of one tetradrachm, is eight days of jury pay. {{CITE:hook-athpol-pay,met}}

    Before you post this
    • The Athenaion Politeia describes the arrangements of its own time, the later fourth century BC. It is not a rate that held for every year, and notably not the rate at the time of the classical owls this site leads with.
    • Its authorship is debated; it is transmitted under Aristotle’s name.
    • Jury service was not daily employment, so eight days of pay is not eight days of life.
    • Rackham’s 1952 translation may still be in copyright; link rather than reproduce at length.
  10. 10PURCHASING POWER

    For a rower in the fleet that sailed for Sicily in 415 BC, one owl was four days at sea.

    Thucydides says the treasury gave a drachma a day to each seaman of that expedition, and that the trierarchs added bounties of their own on top. At four drachmas, one tetradrachm covers four days of the state’s share. {{CITE:hook-thuc-seamen}}

    Before you post this
    • This is the rate for one exceptionally well-funded expedition, which Thucydides presents as unusually lavish. It is not a standing naval wage.
    • Thucydides says captains paid extra, so what a man actually earned varied.
    • The related passage at 3.17.4, where hoplites at Potidaea draw two drachmae a day, is regarded by many editors as a later insertion. Do not lead with it.

Four things we could not verify — so neither should you

These circulate widely and we could not stand any of them up against a source we were able to open and read. They are not on this page because they did not earn their place.

  • A silver purity figure — “about 98% silver”, or any percentage. The relevant XRF study of 788 coins exists, but its abstract gives no purity figure and the full text could not be opened. Post no number.
  • How many owls Athens struck, in total or per year. No mintage estimate from a museum or peer-reviewed source could be located and read.
  • A proportion of surviving owls that are test cut. Only dealer and hobby pages make that claim quantitatively.
  • “One of the most widely used and widely circulating coinages of the ancient Greek world.” Attributed all over the web to the ANS. Both candidate ANS pages were downloaded in full and searched: the sentence is in neither.
